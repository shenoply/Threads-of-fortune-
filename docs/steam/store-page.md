## Long description

**Giza, 1925.** Your father kept a carpet stall in the lane behind the bazaar. He has left you his corner, three rugs and what he owed to Uncle Rashid. Rashid will give you stock, and time, but not much of either.

Threads of Fortune is a trading and life game set in Egypt and the Middle East between 1925 and 1930. You sell at a small stall, one buyer at a time, and every buyer wants something: a size for a room, a colour to please a wife, a story to tell their friends, a price they can boast about. Learn what a carpet is worth, and what it is not, and the bazaar will learn your name.

**Features**

- **The stall.** Read each buyer, show the right rug, and haggle face to face. Regulars remember how you treated them. Dress your corner, hire a boy to mind it, and work your way up from a borrowed mat to a shop in the Khan el-Khalili.
- **The road.** Nearly thirty towns from Alexandria to Constantinople, Baghdad and Isfahan, by Nile ferry, the Egyptian State Railways, camel caravan, steamer and the Nairn desert cars. Prices differ from town to town; so do the dangers.
- **Carpets as they really are.** Fifty-eight kinds of carpet, each with its weave, dyes and tricks of the trade. Count the knots, test the dyes, spot a fake or a moth, and have a doubtful piece examined in a laboratory.
- **Rivals.** A young dealer across the lane who wants your buyers, and the great houses of Cairo, Alexandria, Damascus and Beirut, each with its own temper.
- **Stories that remember.** Five stories run through the years: a forged Mamluk carpet in the Muski, a sealed bale from Port Said, a collector's commission, two weddings that want the same carpet, and a great house brought low by the slump. What you choose stays chosen.
- **The road fights back.** Robbers, smugglers' gangs and old grudges wait on the roads. Hire guards and lead them in short tactical fights, or pay, or talk your way past.
- **Five years of history.** The boom of the late 1920s, the papers, the radio and the crash of 1929–30 change what buyers pay and what banks will lend.
- **Weaving.** Design a carpet of your own and have it woven by the weavers of Kerdasa.
- **A life, not only a ledger.** Your health, your clothes, your household, a marriage, a family to keep, and the coffee house where Bilgin hears everything first.
- **Persia.** Earn a shop in Baghdad and two other cities and a Persian visa, and the road over the Zagros opens to Kermanshah, Hamadan, Tehran, Kashan, Isfahan and Kerman.
- **Ways to play.** The guided campaign, a one-life campaign, and a sandbox with every road open from the first day.

All the people and businesses are fictional. The places, the railways, the prices of the day and the events in the papers follow the history of 1925–30 as closely as we could manage.

## Content descriptors (Steam content survey, draft)

Answer the survey honestly; this is what the game contains.

- **Violence.** Road fights between the player's hired guards and robbers or rival gangs, with swords, clubs and period rifles. Men are "cut down", "shot dead" or wounded, named in the result. Drawn as painted figures; no blood effects, no gore, no dismemberment. The player character can be wounded, fall ill and, in the one-life mode, die. Suggested survey answer: *Some violence* (not "Frequent Violence or Gore").
- **Crime and smuggling.** One story asks the player to carry a sealed bale past the Coastguard for a dealer; the contents are not stated. Cargo jobs include controlled medical drugs (cocaine, opium, laudanum) carried under a chemist's or physician's licence, and the game explains the 1925 Egyptian narcotics law. A chemist's shop mentions morphine and cocaine sold on prescription. There is no drug use by the player.
- **Alcohol and tobacco.** Cabarets and music halls in Cairo and Alexandria have a bar; drinking and champagne are mentioned in text. Characters smoke. No drinking mechanic.
- **Gambling (in-game money only).** Café games (dominoes, backgammon, chess) and a camel race can be played for a stake in game money. No real money, no purchasable currency.
- **Mature themes.** Debt, bankruptcy, illness and death in the family, the 1929–30 crash, colonial-era attitudes voiced by period characters.
- **Sexual content.** None. Cabaret shows are described, not shown in any explicit way.

Suggested descriptor set: **General Mature Content** with the notes above in the description box ("Contains depictions of combat, references to smuggling and to controlled drugs in a historical setting, alcohol and in-game gambling with no real-money element.").

## AI-generated content disclosure (draft)

Steam's content survey asks about AI-generated content that players see or hear and that is used in marketing. Since the January 2026 revision it separates **pre-generated** content (made with AI tools during development, shipped as fixed files) from **live-generated** content (made by AI while the game runs), and it says AI tools used only for efficiency behind the scenes are not its focus. Sources: [PC Gamer, 16 Jan 2026](https://www.pcgamer.com/software/ai/steam-updates-ai-disclosure-form-to-specify-that-its-focused-on-ai-generated-content-that-is-consumed-by-players-not-efficiency-tools-used-behind-the-scenes/), [GamingOnLinux, Jan 2026](https://gamingonlinux.com/2026/01/valve-tweak-steam-ai-disclosure-form-for-developers-to-clarify-its-for-content-consumed-by-players).

**Live-generated content: none.** The game makes no calls to any AI service while it runs. Every picture, line of text and voice line is a fixed file shipped with the game.

**Pre-generated content (draft text for the form; the owner should confirm each line before submitting):**

> Threads of Fortune uses pre-generated AI content, made during development and reviewed by the developer before inclusion. Nothing is generated while you play.
>
> - **Art.** Most of the painted art (town scenes, characters, carpets, the stall, the battle figures, the title paintings and the store images) was generated with an AI image model (ChatGPT image generation) from the developer's written briefs, then selected, corrected, cut out and composited by hand.
> - **Voices.** Several characters' voices are synthetic: stock text-to-speech voices (Kokoro, Piper) that do not imitate any real person, and voices made with a commercial text-to-speech service (ElevenLabs). One character's voice was converted to the voice of a real person who gave his consent. The hero's lines are recorded by a human.
> - **Films.** Short cutscene films are made from AI-generated pictures and footage, with recorded or synthetic narration.
> - **Writing.** The dialogue, letters, newspaper items and stories were drafted with the help of AI writing tools and edited by the developer.
>
> Guardrails: all AI output was reviewed by a person before it went into the game; people and businesses are fictional; no voice imitates a real person without that person's consent; historical claims are checked against period sources and marked as such in the game's notes.

## Price suggestion

**US $14.99** at launch (the Steam price tier closest to the content), with a **launch discount of 10–15%** for the first week.

Reasons:

- The campaign is long (five in-game years, nearly thirty towns, five stories), and the sandbox and one-life modes add replay. Players of trading and management games judge by hours; $14.99 reads as fair for a game of 20+ hours.
- It is a first game from a solo developer, with painted 2D art and no multiplayer. $19.99 asks for more trust than a new name has; $9.99 tells buyers it is small, which it is not.
- $14.99 leaves room for a 25–40% seasonal sale (to $8.99–$11.24) without going below the impulse level.
- The demo carries saves over, so the price is judged after an hour of play, not on the trailer alone.

Use Steam's recommended regional prices as the starting point (see launch-checklist.md); do not undercut them in Egypt, Turkey or the Middle East beyond Valve's suggestion.
