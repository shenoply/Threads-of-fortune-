F  RUG STORIES, CRAFT AND DURABILITY
------------------------------------
F1. This comes up the river with the grain boats. Every house on the Nile has one like it, and every house is right to.
F2. Flatwoven, tight, and the red is a real madder red. Simple work done properly.
F3. Nothing to wear down. Beat it, wash it, turn it over. It will outlast the floor.
F4. A sailor's rug. They buy these at Ismailia and carry them round the world rolled in a sea chest.
F5. The hooks and lozenges are old village patterns. Look at the little gaps where the colours meet: woven by hand.
F6. Flat and tightly packed, no pile to wear through. Feet, chair legs, years of traffic: it takes all of it and shows nothing.
F7. A family rug from the Delta. The kind a man buys when he marries and keeps until his grandchildren sit on it.
F8. Turkish knots, good thick wool, and the medallions are drawn by eye, not from a cartoon. You can see the weaver's hand.
F9. Thick village wool. Children, guests, feasts: it takes all of it.
F10. Your father kept this one back. He said it was woven by a family in the Fayoum who put a little camel in every rug they made. There, in the corner.
F11. Coarse wool, and the red changes shade across the field. That is abrash: a new batch of dye. Machines never do it.
F12. It has already lived twenty years on a stone floor. It will live twenty more.
F13. The dealers call these Red Medina. It is a market name, not a town. The red is what people come back for.
F14. The medallion is an old Anatolian shape, drawn clean. Good red, good blue, and no chemical orange.
F15. Dark red forgives everything: coffee, feet, the street. It will look better in ten years.
F16. For a corridor. You walk in along a line of palm trees, like the road to Saqqara.
F17. Every palm is slightly different. The weaver was counting in her head, not from a paper.
F18. Runners take the most feet in the house. This one has the wool for it.
F19. From the moulid at Tanta. The women there buy light rugs for their receiving rooms and fight over the good ones.
F20. A pale field is hard to weave. One dirty hand and it shows. This one is clean.
F21. For a receiving room, not a kitchen. Shoes off, and it will stay beautiful.
F22. The shipping agents at Port Said put these in their offices. A French captain tried to buy this one off the quay.
F23. Wool on a cotton warp, so it lies flat. The octagons are a trade pattern every weaver from Tabriz to Smyrna knows.
F24. Indigo does not fade. Put it in the sun and it stays blue.
F25. Cairo work. The old Mamluk weavers used stars like these five hundred years ago, and a few workshops near the Khan still do.
F26. Fine wool and a restrained palette, only a few colours. The stars are laid out by count, row by row.
F27. Fine wool is stronger than it looks. In a sitting room it will outlast the house.
F28. The orange growers of Jaffa buy these for their new houses. The rosettes in the border, they say, are oranges.
F29. Three medallions in a column, and the border never breaks at the corners. That takes planning.
F30. Cotton warp, good wool. A family rug that can take a family.
F31. This one has travelled. It crossed the Lebanon on a mule, and someone mended it in Damascus. See the new wool at the edge.
F32. Two traditions in one rug: Syrian stripes, Anatolian stars. Nobody knows exactly where it was woven.
F33. Heavy caravan wool. It has already survived the worst a rug can live through.
F34. Woven in Cairo in 1910. We still have the workshop's receipt, with the stamp. That is rare for a rug this age.
F35. The medallion is a garden seen from above: a pool in the centre, flowers around it. Close knotting for a Cairo piece.
F36. Cotton foundation, so it lies flat and keeps its shape. Fifteen years old and nothing has moved.
F37. Bought in the Old City. The seller said it was made for a house near the Damascus Gate, and prayed on for twenty years.
F38. Quiet colours, the colour of the city's stone. It is meant to be calm, not to shout.

F40. From the plateau near Konya. The villages there weave through the winter, when the snow shuts the roads.
F41. Long, lustrous plateau wool and natural dyes. The angles are sharp because the knots are counted, not drawn.

F52. This came from the Maghreb with a French officer. Nobody in Cairo has another one like it, and collectors know it.
F53. Thick tribal wool and motifs I have never seen on an Egyptian loom. Different hands, different ideas.

F56. Fine wool with a gold-yellow that only comes from weld and patience. Salon quality.

F60. Tough mountain wool, but it is old. Treat it with respect.

F67. Tulips and carnations, the Ottoman court flowers, with silk highlights. Look at the border: it never loses count.
F68. Two centuries of care kept it like this. It deserves the same.
F69. The Persian merchants of Baghdad say it was woven for a prince and carried down the Tigris by night, so nobody would see it leave.
F70. Very fine wool on a fine foundation, close enough that the drawing looks painted. This is the best weaving I have ever held.
F71. It belongs in a palace or a museum. A king should decide where it lies.
